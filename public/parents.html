👪 Child Cruelty Towards Parents

Harsh words and threats from children can leave deep scars on parents too. Respect can turn hurt into healing.

In many homes, children shout at parents, use abusive words, threaten to run away or harm themselves, and publicly shame their parents. These actions may happen in anger, but they still count as emotional cruelty and can damage parents’ mental health. This page helps students understand how their behaviour affects parents, and how to express anger and disagreement in more respectful, balanced ways.

Common Cruel Behaviours

  • Shouting at parents, using abusive language, or mocking them in front of others.
  • Threatening to run away, harm yourself, or call police just to control them.
  • Throwing things, slamming doors, or breaking objects during arguments.
  • Ignoring parents for days, refusing to answer or help, giving “silent punishment”.
  • Insulting parents on social media, WhatsApp groups, or in front of relatives.

Healthier Ways to Show Anger

  • Pause and breathe 5–10 times before replying when you feel like shouting.
  • Use sentences like “I feel stressed when…” instead of “You are always…”.
  • Ask for a short “cool-down break” and continue the talk after everyone is calmer.
  • Write your feelings in a note or message and share it respectfully with parents.
  • Choose a fixed time every week for family discussion about rules and problems.

Your Role in Family Peace

  • Thank parents for efforts like food, fees, or care, even if life is not perfect.
  • Help with daily chores without being asked, especially when they look tired.
  • Share exam stress, friend issues, or social media pressure early instead of exploding later.
  • Ask respectfully for more freedom and explain your reasons instead of threatening.
  • Accept some “no” decisions when they protect your health, safety, or future.

Why Cruelty Towards Parents Happens

  • High pressure from studies, competition, and comparisons with friends or cousins.
  • Copying behaviours seen in movies, series, or reels that show disrespect as “cool”.
  • Unspoken hurts on both sides—past fights, broken promises, or harsh words.
  • Constant use of phones and games reduces real talk time with parents.
  • Difficulty handling anger or disappointment without shouting or threats.

Respectful Habits to Practise

  • Greet them in the morning and when they return home; small manners change the mood.
  • Look at them when they speak instead of always staring at screens.
  • Speak one by one—avoid interrupting or talking over them in anger.
  • Use calm tone and normal volume even when you disagree strongly.
  • Say “sorry” and “thank you” more often; they are powerful relationship tools.

When You Need Extra Support

  • If you feel out of control in anger, talk to a school counselor or trusted teacher.
  • Learn simple anger-management skills: exercise, music, journaling, deep breathing.
  • Suggest family counseling or workshops if your home has daily shouting on both sides.
  • Share with a mature relative who can guide you without taking sides.
  • Remember: asking for help is a sign of responsibility, not weakness.
